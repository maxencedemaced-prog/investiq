How does compound interest work?

With simple interest, you earn the same amount every year, calculated only on your starting amount. With compound interest, the interest you earn is added to your balance and earns interest itself the following year. It’s a snowball effect: slow at first, then stronger and stronger over time.

At 5% a year, 10,000 earns a little over 500 in the first year. By year ten it earns much more, because interest is calculated on a balance that has already grown. After 20 years, compounded monthly, that 10,000 becomes about 27,100 without adding anything.

The compound interest formula

For a single lump sum:

Final amount = Starting amount × (1 + rate)number of periods

If you add money every month, each contribution grows for a different length of time: the first for the whole period, the last hardly at all. The calculator adds it all up for you, with interest compounded monthly (annual rate ÷ 12).

Example: 100 a month for 20 years

You set aside 100 every month for 20 years — 24,000 in total. With a hypothetical average return of 5% a year, you’d end up with about 41,100: more than 17,000 comes from compound interest alone.

Time matters even more than the amount. 200 a month at 5% for 30 years grows to about 166,500. The same 200 for 40 years grows to about 305,200 — nearly double, for only 24,000 more contributed. That’s why people often say: start early, even with small amounts.

Compound interest and dollar-cost averaging (DCA)

Investing the same amount every month, whatever the markets do, is called dollar-cost averaging. It smooths your purchase price over time and avoids trying to guess the “right moment”. Combined with compound growth and reinvested dividends, it’s one of the simplest ways people grow savings over the long term.

The rule of 72

To estimate how long it takes to double your money, divide 72 by the annual return. At 6% a year, money doubles in about 12 years. At 3%, it takes about 24 years.

Frequently asked questions

What rate of return should I use?

It depends on the investment. A savings account pays little but carries little risk. Stocks and broad index funds have historically returned more over long periods, but with sometimes sharp drops along the way. Try several cautious assumptions: past performance is not a reliable indicator of future results.

Are taxes and fees included?

No. The calculator shows a gross return. Depending on the type of account (for example a taxable brokerage account, a retirement account such as an IRA or 401(k) in the US, an ISA in the UK, or a pension plan), taxes and fees reduce the final result. For a more realistic picture, lower the return a little. This is not tax advice.

Is inflation included?

No: amounts are in today’s nominal money. To think in purchasing power, subtract expected inflation from the return (for example 5% − 2% = 3%).
