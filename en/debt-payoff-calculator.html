Avalanche or snowball?

With both methods, you keep making the minimum payment on every debt, and put all the extra money on one debt at a time. When it’s paid off, its payment is added to the extra amount and you move on to the next one: the effort grows at each step.

  • Avalanche: start with the debt that has the highest interest rate, often a credit card or an overdraft. This is the method that costs the least interest.
  • Snowball: start with the smallest balance. It often costs a little more, but clearing a first debt quickly helps you keep going.

What matters most isn’t the perfect method: it’s paying a bit more than the minimum every month, and not taking on new debt at the same time.

Why the minimum isn’t enough

On a credit card, the minimum payment sometimes barely covers the interest: the balance goes down very slowly. Adding even 50 or 100 a month can shorten repayment by several years.

Frequently asked questions

How much does an overdraft cost?

An overdraft usually comes with interest and sometimes fees. Overdraft fees vary by bank and country, and some banks no longer charge them: check your bank’s fee schedule. Keeping a small buffer in your account helps avoid these charges.

Should I save or pay off debt first?

Many people first keep a small reserve (for example one month of expenses), so they don’t slip back into overdraft at the first surprise. After that, paying off expensive debt often saves more in interest than savings would earn. See the emergency fund calculator. This is general information, not personal advice.

I can no longer keep up with my payments. What can I do?

Talk to your lender early: many have hardship options. Free, independent debt advice exists in many countries (for example nonprofit credit counseling in the US, or free debt advice services in the UK and across the EU). Be wary of paid services that promise to make debt disappear.
